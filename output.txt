# The Rules
You'll need a group of at least two players. One of these players will be the Gamemaster (GM). He describes the world and how it reacts to the actions the other players take. The rest of the players each control a character in this world known as an explorer. When this section says 'you', its speaking to those players.

## Attributes
You have four Attributes.
Fitness (FT):: is innate physical power.
Discipline (DS):: is physical training.
Instinct (IN):: is innate mental ability.
Cognition (CG):: is mental training.

Everything roll you'll make in the game uses these Attributes either directly or indirectly. The higher the number is, the better your explorer is with that Attribute. 

## Turns
Action happens in Turns. Simply put, a Turn is the time it takes for you to move and take one action. As long as everyone gets a Turn, and as long as each Turn encompasses roughly the same amount of time, there's no need to get more specific that that. 

This applies both in and out of combat. When the group is exploring, talking, or investigating, the GM should move the spotlight from player to player, giving each a Turn to act before circling back. No single player should keep rolling actions while others wait.

\column

The Actions an Explorer can take will depend entirely on the situation; there's not a comprehensive list. There is a limit to how much you can do at once, though. You could fling open a door as you come to it or break it down. You couldn't however, make an attack, juggle kittens and decrypt a computer all at the same time.

##### Readying Actions
You can ready a conditional action by stating the event that will trigger it and what you want to do in response. If the triggering event happens before your next Turn, you get to take your action before it resolves. If it doesn't, you wait until your next Turn to take an action.

## Throws
Sometimes, the action you want to take will have an obvious outcome, and the GM will narrate what happens. The GM might ask you for more details, but he'll make the decision.

At other times, an action might be dangerous or uncertain. There may be risks involved. When this happens, the GM will ask you to make a Throw.

##### Easy & Difficult
If the rules say a Throw is **Easy**, roll twice and take the result you like; if the rules say a Throw is **Difficult**, roll twice and take the result the GM likes. Easy and Difficult don't stack. A roll is either Easy, Difficult, or normal.

{{pageNumber,auto}}

\page

### Skill Throws
Skills have a Rating from 1 to 6. To make a Skill Throw, roll 1d6 and compare it to your skill's Rating.
- If the result is **equal to or less than** the Rating you succeed.
- If the result is **greater than** the Rating you fail -- or succeed at a cost. 

##### Succeeding at a Cost
If you succeed at a cost, it means that while you did get what you wanted, there's some new complication, or a new faction in play, or perhaps you just owe someone something now. The GM decides the details when needed.

### Usage Throws
Consumable or depletable resources are assigned a Usage Die (e.g. Ud8). When you make a Usage Throw, you roll that die. If you roll a 1 or 2, the die size steps down one. When you roll a 1 or 2 on Ud4, the resource is depleted.

{{note
### GM: Using Usage
Don't track everything. Only track those things that would cause complications when they run out unexpectedly: Ammuni-tion, an NPC's willingness to help the Explorers out... oxygen.

It's okay to say that something has a finite amount of uses if the point isn't to make it dangerous. 
}}

If you make a ranged attack on an In Reach enemy, but the Throw is Difficult.
{note}

\column

## Combat
When you decide to attack something, or when something attacks your character, you're in combat. Combat is conducted in Rounds, in which everyone involved in the combat gets a Turn.

### Combat Throws
Combat Throws always use a d20. They have a rating equal to the Attribute they rely on. Combat Throws against higher-level enemies also have a Toughness equal to the difference in levels between the Explorer and the enemy.
- If your Throw is less than your Attribute and greater than the enemy's Toughness, the Throw is successful.

### Sequence
At the beginning of each Round, each player makes an Instinct Throw.
1. Players that succeed act first, in the order they prefer.
2. GM characters act next.
3. Players that fail act last.

Once everyone has acted, another Round begins with another Instinct Throw.

### Attack
Make a **Fitness** Throw to attack in melee (In Reach). Make a **Discipline** Throw to make a ranged attack (Close or In Range).
- If your Throw successful, roll your damage dice; the GM subtracts the result from the enemy's HP.
- If your Throw is equal to or less than the enemy's Toughness, don't deal damage. Instead, reduce the enemy's Toughness by one.

{{pageNumber,auto}}

\page

##### Damage Types
Every attack has a Damage Type: Acid, Cold, Electric, Fire, Force, Kinetic, or Psionic. An enemy that is Resistant to the type of damage you deal makes your damage throw Difficult; a Vulnerable enemy makes the damage throw Easy. 

##### ONGOING DAMAGE
Substances like acid, poison, or fire can deal ongoing damage. If so, treat your damage dice as if they were Usage Dice. Roll them every turn against that enemy until they deplete, or until the enemy acts to negate the effect.

### Move
Everything has a position which amounts to two things: where it is and its distance to other things. Positioning uses range bands. From near to far:
Reach: ::You're able to make melee attacks against enemies in Reach, and they're able to attack you in melee.
Close: ::You're able to get to something with one move, but it can't attack you in melee yet.
In Range: ::You can make ranged attacks against it and it can make ranged attacks against you.
Distant: ::Far enough away that it'll take you at *least* two moves to get there.

A Move can take you one **range band** closer to or farther from an enemy or ally. You can use your Action to Move a second time. 

##### Breaking Free
When you try to move away from an in Reach enemy, or you move through an enemy's Reach, you must Break Free. Make a **Discipine** Throw. If you fail, choose to stop in the enemy's Reach or give them a free immediate attack. 

\column

##### Interception
When an In Reach enemy tries to move away from you, or tries to move through an area that's In Reach of you, you Intercept. A successful melee attack means the enemy must stop In Reach of you and take damage from the attack.

### Recover
You can Act to recover HP. Spend any number of your HD, roll them, and heal equal to the result. Spent HD are usable again after a full rest.

### SAVING THROWS??
At the end of your turn, make a Saving Throw against each negative condition that affects you. Use Fitness for physical effects or Instinct for mental effects.

{{note
### Other Actions
You can try to do pretty much anything else as well. Other actions will usually require a Skill Throw or a Combat Throw, usually if the action is potentially dangerous.
}}

### Defense
When a threat attacks you, you'll make a **Discipline** Throw to defend in melee or a **Instinct** Throw to defend against a ranged attack. If the Throw is less than your Attribute and greater than the enemy's Toughness, you avoid the attack. Otherwise, it deals damage that you subtract from your HP. 

{{pageNumber,auto}}

\page

##### ONGOING DAMAGE
When you take Ongoing damage, the damage dice are treated as Usage Dice until they're depleted or until you take action to stop the damage (e.g. jumping in water to extinguish flames).

##### Armor
Armor has a Value (AV). You gain a pool of d6s (known as Armor Dice) equal to the AV. If your explorer would take damage, you can spend and roll any number of Armor Dice and reduce the incoming damage by the result, then reduce your Armor's AV by the number of dice you spent.

When you rest, roll each spent Armor Die again. If you get a higher result this time increase your AV by one. Since you can't roll higher than six, any Armor Die that rolled 6 originally can just be discarded.

### Death
When you are reduced to 0 HP, roll 2d6.
- If your result is **higher than the number of Scars you have**, you?re still alive. Gain a new Scar. After Combat is over, you?ll be able to heal.
- If the result is **equal to the number of scars you have or lower**, you succumb to your injuries and die.

If any of your Attributes reach 0, you?re also dead.
{note}

\column

##### NPC Death
When an NPC is reduced to 0 HP, it is incapacitated and can take no further actions. The GM will rule if it is unconcious or dead.

### Ending Combat
Combat ends when one side is down or decides to flee. You must decide as a group with the other players when it makes sense for you to flee. If you decide to flee, narrate how you escape, but expect the GM to make things worse for you later as a result.

If half the enemies in a fight (or the strongest single enemy) are reduced to 0HP, a successful Influence Throw in place of an attack -- with Toughness from the enemy that currently has the most HP -- will cause the enemy side to flee.

When combat ends, explorers at 0 HP regain 1d4 HP as long as you are victorious or they escape with you.

### Resting
Once per day you can take a short rest. Roll your all your unspent HD and recover HP equal to half the result, up to your Maximum HP.

Rest somewhere safe for the night to roll all your HD and recover HP equal to the result, up to your Maximum HP. When you rest safely, you can also roll a 1HD and restore an equal number of points in any reduced Attributes, divided as you like, up to their original value.

## Advancement
Your character advances by gaining experience and then spending that experience to gain new levels. 

### Experience
Experience measures a Explorer's development as they adventure and grow in power. The GM is the final judge of when Experience is due, but should use these examples as a guide:
- You discover a major new threat.
- You complete a mission.
- You overcome, disable, or survive a deadly and powerful threat.
- You succeed or fail in such a spectacular manner that everyone at the table agrees it?s worth it.

### Levelling up
Once you have acquired a number of Experience equal to 2 + your current Level you may spend your next  rest to gain a level. Afterwards, reset your Experience to 0.

When your Hero gains a new level, do these things:
1. Make a Throw for each Attribute. On a failure, gain +1 to that Attribute, to a maximum of 18. The Throw for one Attribute of your choice is Easy.
2. Recheck skills if any of your Attributes increased.
3. Gain an additional Hit Die, then roll all your Hit Dice. If the result is greater than your Maximum HP, the result is your new Maximum HP.
4. Choose whether to Awaken (p. 21).

## Reputation
Reputation is currency, and your identity is your bank. The ancient Earth cryptocurrency craze and China's Social Credit System of the early twenty-first century were the foundations for Zh?ng Securident to birth a new form of financial surety. Backed by every pre-Fall megacorp and most governments -- on Earth and in the colonies -- the Zh?ng Services Exchange was a virtual trading floor when services of all types are valued and traded. That original infrastructure bloc is shattered now, but it's surviving pieces operate much the same. The only difference is now when you travel to a place that has no reliable way to check your reputation, you have to take it with you. 

When you buy something from someone, you digitally sign a gaurantee to render an equal amount of service in return, based on the current CSE rates. If your seller doesn't need those services, they'll list them on the Exchange. Eventually someone will contact you who needs yoru help with something you're good at.

Your explorer's reputation -- and therefore their purchasing power -- is abstracted as a Usage Die, otherwise known as the ?hang Die (?D). It will step up and down in size as you purchase and sell items.

\column

##### Credit Chips
You can convert your CD into a credit chip. This is a one-time use item with a value equal to your Credit Die at the time of conversion. After you convert your credit, you effective credit is zero, unless you sell more items or finish a job for someone -- or re-convert your chip back to normal credit. You'll need to do this when you travel long distances. 

However, you can use your credit chip in one more way. ## Purchasing
To purchase an item, make a Throw using your CD. If your result is greater than the cost, you have enough money to purchase the item. If your result is equal to or less than than the cost, choose one: 
- The item isn't quite what you wanted; the GM will tell you how. Maybe that railgun is stolen, and now the previous owner is after you too. Maybe the guide you hired isn't really much of a guide at all and you get stranded two days into your trek when he decides to pack up quietly at night and go home. 
- You've gone into debt to purchase it; the GM will tell you what you have to do to pay off the debt.
- Step your Credit Die down one step (toward d4).

## Weapons Tech
Pre-Fall technology was impressive both by real-life modern standards and post-Fall standards. They had mastered fundamental physics and begun to tinker with forces probably better left alone. Below are some ideas for weapons, but they need not be the *only* ideas. As long as you and your GM agree on the amount of damage and type of damage that a weapon deals, that's all you need.

Melee weapons have stayed much the same, with the biggest advancement coming in the form of stronger, more durable, lighter materials like carbon nanotubes, metallic glass, and quantum-lattice alloys. These materials are also used in making armor, along with para-aramids and ceramic-steel, as well as more exotic materials such as biophasic, nanoweave, and monocrystal materials.

\column

The simplest ranged weapons still fire physical projectiles; these are known as kinetic weapons. They range in power from ancient firearms that use an explosion to push a bullet to electromagnetic railguns that fire metal bolts at supersonic speeds.

Beyond these, there are laser, plasma, or microwave weapons that damage with heat; rifles that fire microscopic payloads of antimatter that annihilate targets, devices that emit resonant electromagnetic waves to shatter molecular bonds; cryonic weapons that slow and freeze targets; and particle projector cannons that fire relativistic particle beams that induce internal thermal shock and secondary radiation bursts.

## Armor Tech
Defensive technology advanced alongside weapon technology. The simplest armor consists of scavenged scrap or integrated plate systems that rely on mass and structural reinforcement for protection. More sophisticated suits employ para-aramids and ceramic-steel composites that disperse impacts and shatter incoming projectiles.

Advanced armor incorporates exotic materials such as self-repairing biophasic structures, ultra-strong nanoweaves woven from molecular-scale fibers, and monocrystal components grown as single continuous crystals. These materials provide exceptional protection while remaining lighter and more flexible than conventional armor.

\column

The most advanced defenses are active systems rather than passive materials. Fusion-powered armor operates electromagnetic countermeasures and adaptive protection systems. Force armor uses directed fields and confined plasma to weaken or deflect incoming attacks before impact. Singularity armor manipulates gravity, spacetime, or blinkgate-derived micro-wormhole technologies to redirect, absorb, or disperse attacks entirely.

Shields employ many of the same principles. Physical shields rely on advanced materials and structural design, while energy shields use electromagnetic fields, plasma barriers, gravitic distortions, or related technologies to intercept attacks before they reach the user.

# Psionics
At first, it was just those who got 'lost' in the blinkgates that gained psionic ability. But as more gates began opening up across the system, more people are gaining these new powers. These people are referred to as **Awakened**. You have the option to Awaken when creating your character, when gaining a new level, and whenever the GM feels it's appropriate.

To **Awaken** your character, follow these steps:
1. Choose a downside. Lose your choice of 1d3 Fitness, Discipline, Instinct, or HP. If you reduce your HP, you make this reduction **after** determining your HP at each level. *If this reduces your HP or an Attribute to 0, you are dead. Make a new character.*
2. If this is your first awakening, roll for both a Form and a Source on the *Power Words* table. If not, roll for either a Form or a Source.
3. Be sure to check to see if any skills have been affected by your attribute changes.

## Manifesting
To manifest a power, choose a Form and a Source that you know, describe the effect that this combination of words creates, and then make a **Cognition** Throw. If your Throw is less than your Cognition and greater than your target's Toughness (if there is one) you succeed and the effect you described happens immediately and then fades.

##### Power Limits
Manifesting a power quickly puts a few limits on what you can do.
- The power can target one In Reach enemy, ally, or object. 
- If your power has an area of effect (e.g. darkness, or a shield) it covers 5 cubic meters, or is 5 meters long.
- If your power deals damage, it deals 1d6.
- If your power heals. it heals 1d6.
- Any effect ends after 1 round.  

##### Damage Type
If a combination of Words suggests an specific type of damage then your Power deals that type of damage. For instance Fire Barrier could deal Fire damage is someone stepped into it.

##### Unusual EFfects
If the GM feels that any specific combination of Source and Form can't have the effect you're attempting, make the roll with Difficulty. If it still succeeds, it's worked this one time, but will never work again.

##### Power Failure
When your Congnition Throw is equal to or less than the target's Toughness, your power fails, but it's less because you did something wrong so much as the target resisted it.

When your Cognition Throw is greater than your Cognition, there's a Backlash. Roll 1d6 per Backlash you've had since you last had a full rest, and find the result on the *Backlash* table on the next page.

\column

### Extended Powers
If you spend at least one minute manifesting your powers without interruption, the effect you describe can persist up to 10 minutes. If you spend one hour, the effect can last up to a day. You can't extend damage effects.

Alternately, if you wish, you can set a triggering action inside the effect's persistance time. Whatever immediate effect you describe happens when  the triggering action occurs before time runs out. You *can* trigger damage effects.

## Venus
Distance from Sol: ::107.7Mkm (0.72 AU)
Temperature: ::150&deg;C to 220&deg;C
Gravity: ::0.9G
Atmosphere: ::Carbon dioxide with small amounts of nitrogen and oxygen.

Venus was once supposed to become a second Earth. Engineers fought a long, losing battle against a planet that never offered any compromise. Entire fortunes were invested in schemes to cool the atmosphere and reclaim the surface. They all failed.

The collapse of the terraforming program forced humanity to abandon the ground, but they did not abandon Venus entirely. Fifty kilometers above the ground, pressure and temperature were already comparable to those of Earth.

The cloud cities were built around immense toruses that served as both habitat and lift. Scale was the new engineering challenge. Even the smaller settlements required a torus measured in kilometers, its fluoropolymer skin holding a volume of air the size of a Terran city.

There was never a true government of Venus. Mining concerns, investment syndicates, religious communities, and ambitious visionaries founded aerostats of their own, each designed according to its creator's needs and resources. Some were compact settlements intended for a few thousand residents. Others grew into floating nations whose ringworks dominated the horizon.

{{pageNumber,auto}}

\page

Every aerostat followed its own drift pattern through the high-altitude winds. Some traveled in loose trading fleets. Others preferred isolation, spending months without seeing another city.

The Fall changed little about that arrangement. Most cities had always been responsible for their own maintenance, produced much of their own food, and governed themselves without outside interference. Trade became less reliable and spare parts harder to obtain, but Venusians had always depended on themselves for survival. 

##### Aerostat Seven
Population: ::70,000
Diameter: ::24 km

Aerostat Seven was built during the final years of the Venus Terraforming Initiative, when its founders still expected the floating settlements to become temporary waystations above a habitable world. Instead, the city outlived the project. Seven's scale was unprecedented among the early aerostats. Its immense ring encloses a sheltered central expanse lined with residential terraces, public plazas, transit hubs, and docking facilities. A resident can travel for hours without leaving the city.

Control of Aerostat Seven passed through dozens of hands over the centuries. Corporate ownership dissolved into district councils, neighborhood charters, and hereditary trusts, none powerful enough to dominate the whole structure. The city remains technically unified, but most residents identify first with their district and only second with Seven itself. Entire sections of the ring stand unused, maintained by caretakers who preserve infrastructure built for a future population that never arrived.

##### Greenhaven
Population: ::33,000
Diameter: ::19 km

Greenhaven was founded by agricultural investors who recognized a simple reality of Venusian engineering: the volume required to keep a city aloft far exceeded the volume needed to house its residents. Rather than leave most of that space empty, they devoted much of the ring to food production. Orchards, terraced gardens, aquaculture tanks, grain vaults, and seed repositories occupy large portions of the structure, with residential districts woven between them.

The city's economy never depended on a single customer or distant government, which allowed it to weather the Fall with relatively little disruption. Merchant ships still travel considerable distances to purchase Greenhaven produce and establish supply contracts. Over time, the city became the center of a loose commercial network whose members frequently share weather data, shipping schedules, and mutual aid agreements while otherwise remaining independent.

##### Saint Brigid's Lantern
Population: ::8,000
Diameter: ::12 km

Saint Brigid's Lantern began as a remote atmospheric observatory established far from the major settlement orbits. Following the Fall, it become the home of a small by the Fellowship of the Sacred Horizon, a small religious movement that taught that humanity's purpose was not to shape the universe, but to witness it. To the Fellowship, Venus's endless cloudscape was a reminder that man's supposed control over the physical world was an illusion.

{{pageNumber,auto}}

\page

The Fellowship gradually transformed the observatory into a permanent settlement. Pilgrims arrive from across the Solar System, though few remain for long. Life aboard the Lantern is quiet, deliberate, and governed more by tradition than law.

The city has little interest in the affairs of its neighbors. Merchant vessels are welcomed and travelers are offered hospitality, but the Fellowship teaches that the Sacred Horizon reveals itself differently to every soul, making conversion both impossible and unnecessary. As a result, Saint Brigid's Lantern has spent centuries drifting through the Venusian skies in near isolation, observing the world rather than influencing it.

# Threat Index

## Creation
Every monster has a few **Basics**, a **Role**, **Abilities**, and maybe a couple **Tweaks**. Follow the steps below to create new monsters.

### Basics
First, decide how powerful your threat needs to be, and what the first and last levels that your players will encounter it. If it's a small range, say, levels 3 to 5, you can make a single threat in the middle of that range (level 4) and tweak it adjust it's level when needed. For a longer range of levels, you may want to make one version for each tier that your players will meet the threat.

You'll also want to decide the threat's size: minion, normal, or elite.
Minion: ::It comes at you in swarms or waves. Minions overpower explorers by means of shear numbers.
Normal: ::A threat that's somewhere beetween the size of large dog and a horse. They're usually dangerous, but can be somewhat fought one-on-one.
Elite: ::Elites are threats that take more than one explorer to handle, even all of them at once!

Once you've decided on the Threat's level and size, note down the baseline stats below.

### Roles
Every threat has a role and has attacks that complement that role. Choose one from the options below, and create the listed attacks. These are just suggestions, so feel free to modify the listed attacks if you wish. When a role calls for an attack, use the listing on the ***Threat Level & Damage*** table on the previous page.

{{note
### Minions
If your threat is a Minion, that's also its role. Give it one melee or ranged attack, and one attack that works once for every three or four minions there are.
}}

#### Artillery
The Artillery's main attack should be ranged. Give it a second attack that triggers if the first attack hits (or misses, or rolls a certain range of numbers). These attacks can be Area attacks.

After rolling for a Artillery's HP, divide the result by four and subtract this new result from the original (*e.g. If you roll a HP of 12, you would subtract 3 for a total of 9 HP.*)

Artillery raises the size of their damage die by one, so if a normal monster of their level would deal, for instance, 1d8, the artillery would deal 1d10.

\column

{{note
### Area Attacks
An area attack targets multiple enemies at once, but lowers the damage the attack deals per target as seen on the Area Attack Damage Table on the next page.
}}

#### Blocker
Blockers are meant to be in the way, so just give them one melee attack.

After rolling for a Blocker's HP, divide the result by four and add this new result to the original (*e.g. If you roll a HP of 12, you would add 3 for a total of 15 HP.*)

Blockers lower the size of their damage die by one, so if a normal monster of their level would deal, for instance, 1d8, the blocker would deal 1d6.

#### Leader
Leaders usually don't like to be in the middle of the fray, so their main attack should usually be ranged.

Then, choose a second ability based on the following suggestions. If you don't like these specificially, make up one that is similar in power.
- Heal an ally equal to the leader's damage die.
- Melee and Ranged Throws against one ally are made with Difficulty.
- Block and Dodge Throws against one ally are made with Difficulty.

#### Spoiler
Spoilers are here to be dangerous. Give them two attacks. The first should be ranged, to ensure they're always relevent to the battle. The second should be a melee attack or an attack that hinders the explorers:
- If the player fails a Grit throw, give them a negative physical effect (*e.g. knock down, can't move, loses HP per tier*), save ends.
- If the player fails a Will throw, give them a negative mental effect (*e.g. confused, can't attack*), save ends.

Then, give it a power that happens when the spoiler hits a hindered enemy. For instance, it ccould deal an extra damage die, lower the target's damage, or slow the target down.

\column

#### Wrecker
The wrecker likes to hurt everything at once. It's main attack can be either melee or ranged, but it's an Area attack (e.g. one group, 1d3 enemies, all adjacent enemies). Give it a second attack that triggers the same way an artillery's second attack does (hit, miss, number array).

It should also gain an attack that it can use once or twice a battle.

After rolling for a Wrecker's HP, divide the result by four and add this new result to the original (*e.g. If you roll a HP of 12, you would add 3 for a total of 15 HP.*)
